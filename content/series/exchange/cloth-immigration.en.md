## Clothing
Figuring out what clothes to bring was probably the second thing that stressed me out most before departure. The number one source of stress was EP1, obviously. Montreal in January often drops into the negative teens, and with the wind, the feels-like temperature can go down to -20 C or even -30 C. For someone from a subtropical place like me, that was a completely unimaginable temperature range.

Indoors in Montreal, the heating is usually pretty strong, often around 20 C, so people mostly dress in layers. That way you don't overheat the moment you go inside.
Around Christmas, a lot of clothing stores here have major discounts, sometimes even down to 30 to 50 percent off, so for some items like down jackets and snow boots, it can make sense to buy them after arriving.

How cold you feel is obviously subjective. I'm probably on the more cold-sensitive side, so I'll just share what I prepared and how it worked for me.

1. Shoes and wool socks
I bought waterproof hiking shoes from Merrxxx. Make sure they are actually waterproof, not just water-repellent, and ideally they should also have decent grip. My wool socks were from Taobao. They pilled pretty easily, so I don't think the wool percentage was very high.
If the temperature isn't too low, above about -10 C, and you won't be outside for long, under 20 minutes, this combo is okay. But if I had to do it again, I think I'd rather buy snow boots or better wool socks so my toes wouldn't freeze while waiting for the bus or going out.

2. Thermal innerwear
My first choice would definitely be UNIQLO Heattech. There is also UNIQLO in Montreal, but it's much cheaper to buy in Asia.
Some people say wearing thermals indoors gets too hot, but for me, an Extra Warm Heattech layer plus a thin long-sleeve top felt just right indoors.

3. Regular clothes and pants
I personally bought a UNIQLO cashmere sweater and warm pants, but honestly I saw a lot of people on the street just wearing jeans. This really depends on how well you tolerate cold.
Most of your warmth comes from the outermost down jacket anyway, so you don't need to overthink your regular clothes too much. Even hoodies that I normally wore in Taiwan worked fine.

4. Outerwear
I brought the thickest coat I owned from Taiwan, and after arriving I bought a down jacket rated to -17 C from Winners.
Winners is a chain store that sells off-season items from other brands, and sometimes you can find discounted pieces from big brands there. I'll probably write another post later specifically about useful stores.

5. Gloves
My gloves were just a random pair I bought at POYA before leaving. Once the temperature dropped below -15 C, my fingers basically froze. After some online research, I learned one important thing: you should buy mittens, not gloves. Mittens are the kind where your four fingers stay together. Some people might think that's inconvenient because you can't use your phone, but honestly, even so-called touchscreen gloves usually work terribly. You're probably better off just carrying a stylus or something.

6. Scarf
I bought my scarf at MUJI. Once the down jacket is on, your neck actually doesn't feel that cold. I mainly used the scarf to cover my nose and mouth when it got really cold, like below -20 C.
That said, whether it's a scarf or a mask, covering your nose and mouth makes your glasses fog up like crazy, so in the end I didn't use it that often.

7. Beanie
This one was also from Taobao. The main thing is that it should cover your ears, because honestly that's the only part that really feels freezing. When it wasn't too cold, I usually just used the hood on my down jacket instead.

## eTA and Immigration
If your exchange is shorter than six months, you do not need a CAQ, Certificat d'acceptation du Quebec, or a study permit. You only need to apply for an eTA, Electronic Travel Authorization, for travel.
The eTA is applied for online and costs 7 CAD. I heard there are a lot of scam websites, so before applying, make sure you're on the official Canadian government site. After a few hours or a few days, you'll get an email saying your application was approved. When you enter Canada, immigration officers can check your eTA using your passport number.

In theory, you don't need to print anything to prove you have an eTA. But when I departed from Kaohsiung International Airport, the ground staff in Taiwan asked me to show proof of approval. Taiwan airports apparently can't verify eTA status directly by passport number, so just to be safe, I recommend printing the approval email and bringing it with you.

When entering Canada, you first go through an electronic declaration machine. I had read online that medicine needed to be declared, but there was actually no medication option on the machine. Food, however, did need to be declared. After completing the declaration, you get a slip of paper and bring it to the next immigration officer. They ask questions based on what you declared, mainly to make sure the food you're carrying doesn't contain meat.
They will also ask your purpose of entry. Once you say you're an exchange student, they may ask which school and which program. Poly's website recommends printing your admission letter and bringing it with you. Immigration might want to see it, though in my case I wasn't asked for it.
