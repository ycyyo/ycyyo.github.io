## Housing
First of all, read the instructions on [Poly's official website](https://www.polymtl.ca/gopoly/en/looking-accommodations-and-moving-montreal) as early as possible.

Housing options can be roughly divided into two categories: dorms, or renting off-campus with other people.

Getting into Poly's dorm, ZUM, is very hard. You can apply online as soon as the internal selection at your home university is done, so you don't need to wait for nomination or your admission letter. But from what I can tell, no exchange student in recent years has actually gotten in, so you should treat it like buying a lottery ticket and not get your hopes up too much.
If you rent off-campus, expect something like 700 to 1500 CAD per month. Based on what older students said, short-term rentals in Montreal are hard to find, and rental scams are everywhere. If you want to rent, it's safer to fly over first, stay in a hotel or Airbnb for a while, and look for housing in person. Also, in Quebec it is illegal for landlords to charge a deposit, so if someone asks for one, you can just refuse.
Besides the housing resources listed on Poly's website, Facebook groups are also useful. The groups I joined included Taiwanese in Montreal, where people are happy to answer questions, and Logement et colocation UdeM/Poly/HEC, which is specifically for housing around the University of Montreal area. Most listings there are shared apartments near campus.
Also, some short-term Airbnb listings are priced similarly to shared rentals, so Airbnb can be a reasonable option too.

I stayed at Residence Maria Goretti, a residence for women aged 18 to 45. It's within walking distance of campus, there are plenty of supermarkets within about a 20-minute walk, and the monthly rent is much cheaper than renting outside, just a little over 500 CAD. The bathrooms and kitchen are shared, and there are also some common areas like lounges and exercise spaces. The management is stricter than a typical dorm, but overall it was a pretty workable place to live.

## Banking
Before going abroad, I got SinoPac Bank's multi-currency debit card. Here's a quick summary of what I liked about it and the problems I ran into. The benefits below are based on SinoPac's announcement for the period from January 1, 2026 to June 30, 2026. If you're reading this later, go check whether the terms have changed.

Benefit 1: you can exchange money when the exchange rate looks good and keep it in a CAD account. As long as you make at least one purchase with the card in that month, you get three free overseas ATM withdrawals. That said, Canadian ATMs still charge their own handling fee of around 3 to 5 CAD.
Benefit 2: the cashback is 2.5 percent, which offsets the 1.5 percent foreign transaction fee and still leaves you with 1 percent back.
Benefit 3: SinoPac has 24-hour customer service through internet calling, so if anything goes wrong while you're abroad, you can contact them without making an international phone call.

**The annoying part**
Not every ATM can actually withdraw cash from the CAD account. After I asked SinoPac customer service, they told me different ATMs have different implementations. Normally, the ATM should let you choose which currency account to use. But some machines just try to withdraw from your TWD account directly, which means you get charged the TWD-to-CAD conversion fee and also can't use the CAD you exchanged earlier.
I tried more than ten ATMs around the city, including machines from almost every major bank, and only one of them worked. Based on what the customer service representative told me, this is not some rare edge case either. The same problem can happen in other countries and with other foreign-currency accounts too.

In Montreal, you actually don't need much cash in daily life. But if your plan is to withdraw money from your SinoPac foreign-currency account and deposit it into a local account, this issue becomes a big problem. Different ATMs also have their own withdrawal limits. I encountered limits ranging from 400 CAD to 1000 CAD, and SinoPac also has its own rule that a single withdrawal can't exceed 22,000 TWD in equivalent value. Since you get charged the ATM handling fee every time you withdraw, in many cases it's more cost-effective to just pay by card directly.
I also asked customer service whether I could transfer money from my SinoPac foreign-currency account to a Canadian bank account via international remittance. Their answer was that I would need to go to a SinoPac branch in person first and register the Canadian account before I could do that.

As for why you'd want a local bank account at all, honestly, you might not need one unless you have a specific reason. Almost everywhere in Montreal accepts cards, but a few places only accept cash or a local debit card. I needed a local account because the place I lived only accepted rent payments through Interac e-Transfer.
Interac e-Transfer is one of the most common transfer methods in Canada, but only accounts at a few major Canadian banks support it. Also, the school insurance fee could only be paid in cash or through a local bank account.

Most exchange students seem to open an account at either Scotiabank or TD. I chose TD. The process was simple. You need to book an appointment online first. I got lucky and was assigned a staff member who spoke Chinese. Basically I only needed to bring my eTA, passport, and school admission letter, and they helped me open a student checking account. After that, I got a local debit card.
The only [TD branch](https://maps.app.goo.gl/xZep5SfDg6SfFFak8) near school had an ATM that could successfully withdraw cash from my SinoPac account, but that ATM was only available during the bank's business hours.
